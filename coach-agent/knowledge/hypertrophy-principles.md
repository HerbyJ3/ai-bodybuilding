# Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training — Knowledge Notes

Each section is a retrieval chunk. Tags show the chapter each chunk comes from.

## [ch1] Specificity
Every part of training should serve the goal, directly or indirectly. It's the top principle: if the wrong muscles are being trained, nothing else can rescue the program. Training goals compete with each other. The more goals someone chases, and the more different they are (hypertrophy and endurance, for example), the less each one progresses. Interference grows when two types of training load the same structures, differ a lot as stimuli, demand a lot of calories, or are very fatiguing. Program design starts with a needs analysis: what the goal requires, and where this person is strong or weak against it.

## [ch2] Overload — what drives growth
- **Growth drivers, roughly in order of importance:** tension, volume, effort (proximity to failure), range of motion, metabolites, cell swelling (pump), mind-muscle connection, movement speed, and muscle damage.
- **Effort.** The reps within about 5 of failure do most of the growth work, because that's when the biggest motor units are fully recruited. This is true at any load in the growth range. Going to failure maximizes stimulus but piles up fatigue, so a mesocycle average around 2–3 RIR gives the best long-term tradeoff.
- **Load and reps.** Loads from roughly 30% to 85% of 1RM all work. Per set, anything from about 5 to 30 reps gives a similar stimulus when taken close to failure.
- **Range of motion.** Use a range that puts a noticeable stretch on the muscle and goes as far as is safe. Depth that reaches a real stretch matters; extreme depth beyond that adds little.
- **Volume.** Train between MEV and MRV. For a typical intermediate that's roughly 2–12 sets per muscle per session across 2–4 sessions a week, with wide individual variation.
- **Training styles.** Straight sets, high-rep sets, myoreps, and drop sets all work. They differ in how much they emphasize tension, pump, or metabolites. Short-rest techniques help when time is short or joints are beat up.

## [ch2] Finding starting volume (MEV estimator)
Start each muscle at a set count you believe is near its MEV, erring low. After the session, rate three things on a 0–3 scale: how hard the target muscle was challenged, how much pump it got, and how disrupted it felt afterward. Add the scores.
- **0–3:** below MEV, so add a few sets next week.
- **4–6:** at or just above MEV, the right place to start.
- **7–9:** overshooting, so cut volume next week.

## [ch2] Progressing volume (set progression)
Each week, per muscle, rate two things:
- **Soreness (0–3):** whether it healed early, on time, or hadn't healed by the next session.
- **Performance (0–3):** whether the user beat this week's target easily, just met it, needed to go past the target RIR, or couldn't match last week.

The decision rule:
- Recovered early and performing well → add sets (more sets the better both scores are).
- Recovered just on time, or performance merely on target → hold volume.
- Performance falling short of last week → stop adding sets and use fatigue management, regardless of soreness.

Score performance as an average across all sets. Week 1 performance always counts as baseline. The body adapts to added volume faster than to added load, so most of a mesocycle's progression comes from sets.

## [ch2/ch6] Load, rep, and RIR progression
- **RIR** runs from about 4–5 in week 1 down to 0–1 in the final accumulation week.
- **Load progression:** add only enough weight to repeat last week's reps at the same or slightly lower RIR.
- **Rep progression:** add only enough reps to keep the same weight at the same or slightly lower RIR.
- **Don't trade volume for load.** Pushing load fast while holding sets low gives great end-of-meso numbers but less growth.

## [ch3] Fatigue management
- Fatigue is the unavoidable cost of stimulus. It shows up locally (in the muscle), systemically, and axially (lower back fatigue affects many lifts).
- **Performance is the best fatigue signal.** If someone can still hit progressive targets, they aren't too fatigued. Act on consecutive drops, or on a drop paired with other signs.
- **Stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR).** Score stimulus as pump, mind-muscle connection, and disruption. Score fatigue as joint stress, perceived effort, and performance loss in muscles not being trained. Divide stimulus by fatigue.
- **Prevention:** stay within volume landmarks, use safe technique, and alternate harder and easier days.
- **Relief, from smallest to largest:** rest days → recovery session → deload → active rest phase.
- **Recovery session:** train the lagging muscle at maintenance volume, possibly with lighter loads. This pauses growth so fatigue can drop, letting a planned mesocycle finish instead of deloading early. It's also useful for a suspected minor tweak or for illness without fever. If the fatigue is systemic or in big muscles, make the whole session a recovery session.
- **Deload:** a full week, used when several muscles have hit MRV, or RIR has reached 0–1. Too early beats too late. In the first half, keep the loads and halve the sets and reps. In the second half, halve the sets, reps, and loads. Exercises can be last meso's, next meso's, or low-fatigue picks. A week this short doesn't cost muscle.

## [ch4] SRA and frequency
Training triggers both growth and fatigue, and each needs time to finish. Growth usually completes in about 1–3 days, but fatigue often clears more slowly, so recovery sets frequency. That works out to about 2–4 sessions per muscle per week, with smaller muscles tolerating more. Too little frequency forces huge sessions: more damage, junk volume, and failing technique. Too much frequency gives sessions too small to build momentum, wastes time on warm-ups, and can outpace connective tissue recovery or systemic MRV.

## [ch5] Variation
- Variation prevents staleness and overuse injury. It's a refinement that only pays off once the other principles are handled.
- Use about 1–3 exercises per muscle per session and 2–4 per week. Keep exercises fixed within a mesocycle and rotate them between mesocycles based on staleness, pain, or stalled performance.
- **Choose exercises by the current limiter:**
  - **RSM (raw stimulus):** best when growth is lagging and recovery and time are fine.
  - **SFR:** best when recovery is the limiter.
  - **STR (stimulus per time):** best when time is short.
  - The ideal exercise scores well on all three.
- Using the whole effective rep range across a career likely builds the most muscle, organized logically across mesocycles.

## [ch6] Phase potentiation
- Each phase should improve the next one and avoid losing earlier gains.
- **Within a training block:** start heavier, lower-volume, and lower-frequency. Shift toward higher volume, higher frequency, and lighter loads in later mesocycles, as MEV and MRV both climb by a few sets per week.
- **Between blocks:** a short low-volume maintenance phase at MV, with low frequency, lets connective tissue recover and restores sensitivity to volume.

## [ch7] Individualization
- Individualization adjusts every other principle to the person: genetics, training age, diet phase, available time, sleep, and stress.
- **Beginners:** technique, lower volume, and a few big exercises.
- **Advanced lifters:** more volume and possibly frequency, more targeted exercises, closer fatigue management, and specialization phases where non-priority muscles drop to MV–MEV.
- **Gain phase:** MV and MEV drop while MRV rises, so the user can afford more high-stimulus, high-fatigue exercises.
- **Cut phase:** MEV rises while MRV falls, and most non-beginners build roughly no muscle. Stay near MEV, especially late in a cut. Lengthen the mesocycle with smaller set additions rather than shortening it, because each deload week is a week of slower fat loss.
- **Maintenance phase:** low-volume work, a good time for efficient training and high-stimulus exercises.
