# Renaissance Diet 2.0 — Knowledge Notes

Each section is a retrieval chunk. Tags show the chapter each chunk comes from.

## [ch1] Diet priorities
Diet variables have unequal impact. Calorie balance is about half the effect and macronutrients about a third. Timing, food composition, and supplements/hydration together make up the rest. A diet built around a minor factor while ignoring a major one underperforms or fails. All of it is multiplied by adherence: a perfect plan that isn't followed produces nothing.

## [ch2] Calorie balance
Calories are the strongest lever on bodyweight, composition, and performance. Building muscle and losing fat pull in opposite directions, so they are run as separate phases: a surplus for gaining, a deficit for losing. Calorie needs differ between people and from day to day for the same person.

## [ch3] Macronutrients
- **Protein** matters most for body composition. The practical default is about 1 g/lb. Needs rise in a deficit because muscle breakdown is higher (minimum about 0.8 g/lb, more on long or aggressive cuts). Needs fall in a surplus (minimum about 0.7 g/lb, though that's sufficient rather than optimal).
- **Carbs** fuel training and support anabolism, so intake should scale with training workload.
- **Fat** is needed for health but does little for performance or composition beyond its minimum (about 0.3 g/lb). Above that floor it's flexible and set by preference.

## [ch4] Nutrient timing
Timing is a real effect but a small one. Spread protein across roughly 4–8 meals, each carrying a modest share of the day's total. Make a meal bigger and slower-digesting when a long gap follows it. Concentrate carbs around training. Push fats away from training and toward long gaps such as sleep or work. A slow-digesting protein makes a good last meal of the day. Never sacrifice total calories, macros, or sleep for timing tricks.

## [ch5] Food composition
Protein quality comes from its essential amino acid profile and digestibility. Carb quality comes mainly from digestion speed plus fiber and micronutrients. For fats, prioritize monounsaturated, then polyunsaturated, then saturated, and keep trans fats minimal. A varied diet covers micronutrients, but a multivitamin is reasonable in a deficit or when a food group is restricted.

## [ch6] Supplements and hydration
Most supplements do nothing measurable, and even the good ones have small effects. The best-supported are caffeine, whey, casein, creatine, carb drinks, multivitamins, and omega-3s. Drinking to thirst covers most people. Heavy sweating or heat calls for a deliberate plan with electrolytes. Drinking a lot of plain water can be dangerous because of low blood sodium. Urine color and before/after-training bodyweight are simple hydration checks.

## [ch7] Adherence
Adherence drives outcomes. Schedule diets away from travel, competitions, and major life disruptions. Choose diet length and aggressiveness based on what the person can realistically tolerate. Motivation builds in stages over a diet; the book's sequence is inspiration → motivation → intention → discipline → habit → passion. Focusing on long-term goals and an internal sense of control helps through the rough stretches.

## [ch8] Hunger management
- **In a cut:** more protein, liquids before meals, high-fiber foods, less palatable food choices, caffeine.
- **In a gain:** protein near its minimum, liquid calories, less fiber, tastier foods.
- Add these strategies gradually as the phase goes on, only when needed.

## [ch9] Nutritional periodization
- **Cuts:** lose roughly 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week for 6–12 weeks. Run at least about 3 weeks so trends are readable. Past about 12 weeks the risk of burnout and rebound climbs, and by 16 weeks it's high. A successful shorter cut beats an overreaching longer one.
- **Gains:** gain roughly 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week for 6–16 weeks. Beginners can run longer, advanced lifters shorter. Gaining faster worsens the muscle-to-fat ratio.
- **Maintenance:** between cuts, maintain for about two-thirds to the full length of the preceding cut, and longer as repeated cycles stack up.
- **Long-term muscle building** cycles gain → maintenance → cut. **Long-term fat loss** alternates cuts with maintenance until the goal is reached.
- **Signs a cut has run too long:** rising hunger, fatigue, worse skill learning, and adherence lapses.

## [ch10] Designing the diet
1. **Estimate maintenance calories** from bodyweight and the day's training class (non-training, light, moderate, hard). Classify days honestly, since people overestimate burn. If unsure, round toward the lighter class in a cut and the harder class in a gain.
2. **Set protein first** (about 1 g/lb).
3. **Set carbs by day type.** Each training class has a minimum and a starting value.
4. **Fill the remaining calories with fat,** then check it stays at or above about 0.3 g/lb. If it doesn't, shift calories from carbs to fat.
5. **Carbs and fat are interchangeable** within limits. Performance-focused users bias toward carbs on training days, while general fitness users can follow preference.
6. **Lay out meals:** roughly 4–6 per day. Place the morning, bedtime, pre- and post-workout meals first, then spread the rest. Divide protein evenly. Put higher carbs and lower fat in meals near training. Choose food quality and supplements last.

## [ch11] Tracking and adjusting
- **Weigh-ins:** 2–3 times per week under the same conditions. Compare weekly averages and the average week-to-week change. Never react to a single weigh-in, and wait for at least 2 weeks of data before calling a trend. Water swings (salt, hard sessions, menstrual cycle) can hide tissue change for a week.
- **Maintenance target:** weight stays within about ±1.25%, with average weekly change near zero.
- **Adjustment math:** about 3,500 kcal per lb of tissue. Convert the weekly weight gap into a daily calorie change, then into grams of a macro.
- **Adjusting a cut:** take calories from fat first (down to its minimum), then from carbs. Leave protein alone. Hitting both floors usually means the cut is near its end anyway.
- **Adjusting a gain:** add carbs first because they're more anabolic. Switch to adding fat once the volume of carbs makes eating hard.
- **Cut → maintenance:** jump to the midpoint between current intake and estimated maintenance. If weight holds, step calories up about 20% every 3–4 weeks. If weight is still falling, add back the measured deficit. If it's rising, hold until stable.
- **Gain → maintenance:** if still gaining, remove the surplus implied by the last two weeks of gain.
- **Mini-cut:** a short, slightly faster cut between gain phases, started from current intake.

## [ch12] Body composition
Bodyweight drives week-to-week decisions, while periodic body-composition tests show muscle versus fat over longer periods. The book gives general health ranges of about 10–20% body fat for men and 15–30% for women. DEXA is the most accurate accessible method. No single measurement is conclusive; look for agreement across methods and over time.
